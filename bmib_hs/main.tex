\begin{abstract}
Preoperative blood tests decide whether an ovarian mass needs cancer
surgery; a model that degrades silently at a new hospital can delay
surgery or trigger unnecessary operations. Eight models---three
published rules (a CA125 cutoff, ROMA, CPH-I) and
five fitted models (a logistic regression, an integer scorecard,
three tree ensembles)---were trained or fixed on a Chinese
cohort (349 patients) and applied, unchanged, to two other
Asian cohorts (West China, 380; Japan, 177). On the 100 West China
patients with measured biomarkers (63\% cancer prevalence), the
scorecard (AUROC 0.971) was numerically highest, significantly
exceeding CatBoost (0.910) and Random Forest (0.930) after
multiple-comparison correction but not the logistic regression
(0.941), ROMA (0.965), or XGBoost (0.946). The logistic regression
tied or beat the scorecard on the imputed cohort (0.936 vs.\ 0.929),
under multiple imputation (0.926 vs.\ 0.853), and under measurement
noise; on the imputed cohort the scorecard missed 47 of 188 cancers
at its fixed high-risk tier versus 82 (ROMA, an imputation artifact)
and 48 (CPH-I) at their
published cutpoints. A simulation (illustrative counterexample only)
showed the flexible models' advantage diminishing and
reversing under simulated drift. Within these small cohorts
the evidence therefore supports parity: the scorecard matched the
linear models and outperformed two of the three tuned ensembles on
the complete
cases---an outcome-selected subset skewed toward high-marker cancers,
so that margin may reflect case mix, not algorithmic
superiority---and its distinctive asset is hand-computability at
the bedside, not predictive
superiority; generalization requires larger validation
studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ovarian cancer is the most lethal gynecologic cancer, and most cases
are found late. When a pelvic mass is found, referral to a specialist
cancer surgeon depends on preoperative risk, and errors in either
direction are costly. That decision uses two cheaply measured blood
proteins, CA125 (cancer antigen 125) and HE4 (human epididymis
protein 4), combined by fixed formulas such as the Risk of Ovarian
Malignancy Algorithm (ROMA)~\cite{moore2011} and the Copenhagen Index
(CPH-I)~\cite{karlsen2015}, or by a fitted model. Machine learning
models have been proposed for the same task, usually with strong
results on training data but little external testing;
\cite{christodoulou2019} found no performance benefit over logistic
regression across clinical prediction models.

Lu et al.~\cite{lu2020} trained machine-learning models on the
Changzhou cohort reused here, selecting HE4 and carcinoembryonic
antigen (CEA) for a decision tree that beat ROMA and logistic
regression on a held-out split. Our work adds a hand-computable
scorecard, applies every model frozen to two Asian cohorts, and asks:
does model complexity produce performance that survives a move to a
new hospital, and can a bedside-computable scorecard match the
logistic regression and the tree ensembles? Interpretability
arguments favor such simple models in high-stakes
medicine~\cite{rudin2019}; we measured discrimination,
calibration, and decision consequences, plus a simulation of when
complex models help.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}

Three public, anonymized datasets were used
\cite{dataset1,dataset2,machida2023} (IRB approval was not required
for re-analysis of public, de-identified data). The cohorts are a
training cohort from Changzhou,
Jiangsu, China (349 patients; 171 ovarian cancers, 178 benign masses;
Mendeley Data~\cite{dataset1}, \url{doi:10.17632/th7fztbrv9.11}, from
Lu et al.~\cite{lu2020}), a
second Chinese
cohort from the West China Second University Hospital, Chengdu
(380 patients; 188 cancers, 192 benign cysts;
figshare~\cite{dataset2}, \url{doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.28831256}), and a
Japanese cohort (177 patients; 27
cancers, 150 healthy women; Karger figshare~\cite{machida2023},
\url{doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.24235450}). Each patient has four features:
CA125
(U/mL), HE4 (pmol/L), age, and menopausal status (recorded in all
three cohorts);
\texttt{src/clean\_data.py} harmonizes the source files into the
processed files used by every analysis. CEA, Lu et al.'s key
feature~\cite{lu2020}, is absent from the two external cohorts, so
fitted models use the four common features.
Scattered training missing
values (17 CA125, 20 HE4) were filled by class-stratified
$k$-nearest-neighbor imputation ($k=\min(5,\text{class size})$,
applied once before modeling). Externally, HE4 was not measured for 74\% of West China
patients and age was not recorded for 85\% of Japan patients; these
external gaps were filled with the frozen training-set medians
(HE4 54.5 pmol/L, CA125 49.3 U/mL, age 45.0 years), so no
validation statistic is used for imputation.
Because the Japanese cohort contains 150 healthy women and only 27
cancers, and performance depends on the base rate among
patients tested~\cite{rolfsen2020}, it is a
specificity stress test, not external validation; its AUROC is
never pooled with West China's. The primary
West China analyses use the 100 patients with measured HE4 (63 cancers,
37 benign); the datasets do not record why HE4 was
measured only there. Relative to the
complete cases, the 280 excluded
patients (125 cancers, 155 benign) had similar age (42.2 vs.\
45.2 years) and menopausal rates (31.8\% vs.\ 31.0\%), lower
CA125 (median 60.7 vs.\ 95.1 U/mL), and lower cancer prevalence:
44.6\% versus 63.0\% (Fisher exact test
$p=0.002$). The complete-case benchmark is therefore selected on the
outcome, and all of its estimates are complete-case estimates at 63\%
prevalence. The 74\%-imputed cohort ($N=380$) is a sensitivity
analysis for the fitted models, but for ROMA it is not a valid
evaluation: with HE4
fixed at one training median, premenopausal ROMA moves only
$0.0626\times\ln(5000/5)=0.43$ logits across a 1{,}000-fold CA125
change, and none of the 191 imputed premenopausal patients crosses
the 13.1\% cutoff at any observed CA125 value---ROMA's
imputed-cohort AUROC
and operating points are imputation artifacts, reported only for
completeness. No-HE4 refits and MICE AUROCs
bound this effect (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}).

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}

The five fitted models used the same four features; the three
published rules use their published inputs (ROMA: HE4, CA125,
menopause; CPH-I: HE4, CA125, age; CA125 rule: CA125 alone).
(1) \emph{Integer
scorecard}:
each continuous feature was rank-transformed and cut into training-set
tertiles (frozen cutpoints: CA125 24.3/165.0~U/mL, HE4 45.8/92.7~pmol/L,
age 37.0/52.0~years), then one-hot encoded and fitted with an
L2-regularized logistic regression ($C=1.5$); each coefficient $\beta_i$
was then rescaled to the nearest whole number
$w_i=\mathrm{round}(5\times\beta_i/\max_j|\beta_j|)\in\{-5,\ldots,+5\}$,
with $|\beta_i|<0.01$ set to zero. The score is the sum of these feature
points (range $-7$ to $+9$: CA125 $-1/0/+2$, HE4 $-4/-1/+5$, age
$-2/0/+2$, menopause $0$, a single bin because the feature is
binary), can be computed by hand, and contains
seven fitted integer parameters
(two, three, and two nonzero bin weights for CA125, HE4, and age,
respectively; the intercept rounds to zero and is unused). HE4 spans
$-4$ to $+5$ versus $-1$ to $+2$ for CA125, matching HE4's cancer
specificity and CA125's elevation in endometriosis; the menopausal
coefficient rounded to zero; the fixed high-risk
tier is score $\ge +1$, and a training-fitted logistic map converts
scores to probabilities, both frozen before external evaluation.
(2) \emph{Logistic regression} on standardized features (no log
transform; CA125 and HE4 standardized on the raw scale). (3--4) the
published formulas \emph{ROMA} and \emph{CPH-I}, computed on raw values:
premenopausal ROMA is $-12.0+2.38\ln(\mathrm{HE4})
+0.0626\ln(\mathrm{CA125})$ (postmenopausal coefficients $-8.09$,
1.04, 0.732), logistic-transformed, with published cutoffs 13.1\%
(pre) and 27.7\% (post)~\cite{moore2011}; \mbox{CPH-I} is $-14.0647
+1.0649\log_2(\mathrm{HE4})+0.6050\log_2(\mathrm{CA125})
+0.2672\times\mathrm{age}/10$, likewise logistic-transformed, with
cutoff 7\%~\cite{karlsen2015}. Units: HE4 pmol/L, CA125 U/mL, age
years; within-cohort recalibration
means fitting a logistic regression of the outcome on the model's
output (score or probability) as the sole covariate---a monotone
rescaling---on West China.
(5) a single \emph{CA125 $\ge$ 35 U/mL} rule. (6--8) \emph{XGBoost}~\cite{chen2016},
\emph{CatBoost}~\cite{prokhorenkova2018}, and \emph{Random Forest}~\cite{breiman2001}, evaluated under two
settings: Setting A keeps each package's default hyperparameters, and Setting B tunes each
ensemble by nested cross-validation, with all tuning decisions confined
to training folds (XGBoost 100 trees, depth 3; CatBoost 500 iterations,
depth 4; Random Forest 500 trees, depth 6).
The primary comparison uses Setting B (Table~\ref{tab:auroc},
Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}); Setting A values are in its
caption; the five fitted models were trained once on the Chinese
cohort and
frozen before any external evaluation (the published rules are fixed
by definition).

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

Discrimination was measured by the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), with
95\% confidence intervals and paired bootstrap tests between
models (2{,}000 patient-level resamples). The prespecified primary
comparison is the scorecard versus ROMA on the 100 complete cases; the
same comparisons on the 74\%-imputed cohort are sensitivity analyses.
All other comparisons are secondary, and effect sizes with
95\% CIs are emphasized over p-values, with Holm--Bonferroni adjustment
across the six comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:discrimination}.
Calibration was measured with the Brier
score, calibration-in-the-large (CITL; the logit of the mean
predicted risk minus the logit of the observed prevalence, negative
when under-predicting), the calibration slope (the logistic
coefficient of the outcome on the logit of predicted risk;
1 $=$ perfect), and the expected calibration error (ECE), the mean
absolute difference between observed and predicted risk across ten
equal-width probability bins~\cite{vancalster2019}; frozen calibration
on the 100 complete cases is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. Decision value used a weighted-harm
analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:results})~\cite{vickers2006}: thresholds were swept over 1--60\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cons} sweeps 1--99\%),
and for exchange rates $w\in\{1,5,10,50\}$ the threshold minimizing
$H=w\times\text{missed}+\text{unnecessary}$ was identified;
for a perfectly calibrated model this minimizer is $t=1/(1+w)$
(50\% down to 2.0\%), probing from indifference ($w=1$) toward
weighting missed cancers heavily ($w=50$).
Q1 asks whether each model transports unchanged; Q2 how well it
performs after local recalibration. The weights
are hypothetical scenarios, not validated clinical utilities; all
design choices (features, primary comparison, weights) preceded
external evaluation, and only the scorecard's bin count and
regularization
were selected among
12 candidates by repeated CV
(Section~\ref{sec:internal}). The primary harm comparison applies
within-cohort recalibration to all five models, so none gains from
refitting on the validation outcome; published-cutpoint
operating points are reported separately.
The retraining experiment used stratified training subsets ($n=60$
to 349, 10 draws), evaluated unchanged on the imputed West China
cohort. Robustness was tested with 10\% and 30\%
multiplicative, additive, and combined noise, 5\% gross-error
contamination, and $\pm10\%$ bin-boundary shifts; the noise grid,
retraining, and no-HE4 refits use the ensembles at package defaults
(Setting A). A MICE-style check imputed West China's features by
chained-equation regression (10 iterations, outcome excluded, one
dataset).
Analyses used Python 3.13 with scikit-learn 1.7.2, XGBoost 3.3.0, and
CatBoost 1.2.10 (seed 42, stratified five-fold
cross-validation, 20 simulation replicates); Setting
A (defaults) is XGBoost 100 trees depth 6, CatBoost 1{,}000
iterations depth 6, Random Forest 100 trees uncapped. Following calls for
released, reproducible pipelines~\cite{roberts2021}, our pipeline
(\texttt{reproduce.sh}, pinned dependencies) is
available at \url{https://github.com/angelayaoo/Ovarian-Cancer-RiskPrediction}.

\subsection{Simulation}

The simulation is an illustrative counterexample, not an explanation
of the observed real-world results: it varies drift ($d$) independently of
covariate shift ($s$) and noise. A simulation calibrated to the Chinese
data drew labels from a mixture distribution and biomarkers from the
corresponding class-conditional lognormal distributions as a proposal,
then generated the outcome from the logistic model
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\eta(z_1, z_2, z_3) = {}&-0.65 + 1.8z_1 + 1.0z_2 + 0.5z_3\\
&+ (1-d)\bigl(1.6z_1z_2 + 1.2\sin(1.7z_1)\bigr),
\end{split}
\label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
where $z_1$, $z_2$, and $z_3$ are the standardized log CA125, log HE4,
and age, respectively, and $d\in[0,1]$ sets the drift level: $d=0$ keeps
the interaction and nonlinear terms, $d=1$ removes them on the
test side, and intermediate values scale them by $1-d$, as if the
biomarker--cancer relationship partially changed between hospitals. The
grid crossed training sizes $n\in\{100,200,400,800\}$, shifts
$s\in\{0,0.5,1.0\}$, noise $\{0\%,50\%\}$, and drift
levels $d\in\{0,1\}$, with
20 replicates per cell. Shift $s$ shrinks class-conditional means toward the pooled mean
and inflates variances (less separation); noise adds Gaussian error
of 50\% of each biomarker's standard deviation to CA125 and HE4.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Discrimination}\label{sec:discrimination}

On the 100 West China patients with measured HE4 (63 cancers), the
scorecard was numerically highest, with ROMA, tuned XGBoost, and the
logistic regression not significantly different in paired bootstrap
tests
(Table~\ref{tab:auroc}, West CC column; Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}).
This benchmark is selected on the outcome (63.0\% vs.\ 44.6\%
cancer prevalence; Fisher
$p=0.002$), and 0.971 exceeds the scorecard's internal CV AUROC of
0.906 (Section~\ref{sec:internal}); it is a complete-case estimate
at 63\% prevalence on a cancer-enriched subset
(median CA125 95.1 vs.\ 60.7 U/mL), not evidence of superior
external performance.
On the 100 complete cases, the primary
comparison gave a scorecard-minus-ROMA difference of $+0.006$ AUROC
(95\% CI $-0.019$ to 0.032; $p=0.65$); the scorecard also did not
differ from the logistic regression ($+0.030$; $p=0.09$) or XGBoost
($+0.024$; $p=0.08$), and exceeded CPH-I ($+0.040$; $p=0.03$),
CatBoost ($+0.058$; $p=0.004$), and Random Forest ($+0.040$;
$p=0.006$); after Holm--Bonferroni, CatBoost (0.02) and
Random Forest (0.03) remained significant. (Paired differences are
bootstrap means, not differences of Table~\ref{tab:auroc} means.)
On the imputed cohort
(sensitivity analysis), the differences
all favored the scorecard (vs.\ ROMA $+0.043$---an
imputation artifact---CPH-I
$+0.061$, XGBoost $+0.040$, CatBoost $+0.052$, Random Forest
$+0.034$; all CIs excluded zero) except the logistic regression
($-0.007$; $p=0.48$), which was highest there (0.936 vs.\ 0.929;
ensembles 0.877--0.895).

In the Japan specificity stress test ($n=177$), CPH-I was highest
(0.920; Table~\ref{tab:auroc}, Japan column; its age is the frozen
training median for 151/177 patients). As a specificity stress test, its key operating points are
false-positive rates among the 150 healthy volunteers at the fixed
cutpoints: 0/150 (scorecard tier, ROMA), 1/150 (0.7\%, CPH-I),
10/150 (6.7\%, CA125 rule).
Within Japan, 85.3\% of healthy women but 18.5\% of cancer
patients fell into both markers' lowest frozen bins; volunteers
had far lower markers than West China patients (median
CA125 13.9 vs.\ 65.1 U/mL; HE4 32.5 vs.\ 54.5 pmol/L, the
latter the frozen imputation value).

\subsection{Decision consequences}
\label{sec:results}

In the primary weighted-harm analysis (Q2; Table~\ref{tab:harm},
Fig.~\ref{fig:cons}), all five models were within-cohort
recalibrated, so no model gains from refitting on the validation
outcome. The logistic regression's minimum harm was lowest up to
$w=10$ (9.7, 29.5, 39.7); at $w=50$, recalibrated ROMA was lowest
(45.8), then the logistic regression (46.3), CPH-I (50.3), and
XGBoost (50.5); minima are over the swept 1--60\% thresholds and can
exceed treat-all (threshold 0), as the scorecard does at $w=50$
(59.5).
Among the three recalibrated rules the scorecard was lowest
up to $w=10$ (12.1 vs.\ 14.7/15.0; 45.0 vs.\ 45.8/50.3)
and fell behind at $w=50$ (59.5 vs.\ 45.8/50.3).
Recalibration barely moved the logistic regression (frozen
9.7/29.2/39.7/46.6) but transformed XGBoost at $w=10$/$50$ (66.1/215.5
vs.\ 50.5/50.5), so a
frozen-versus-recalibrated comparison would have been unfair to
XGBoost. All Table~\ref{tab:harm}/Fig.~\ref{fig:cons} quantities are
per 100 women at 49.5\% prevalence (treat-all $=$
192/380 $=$ 50.5)~\cite{rolfsen2020}; the formula rows use the
imputed cohort, where ROMA's input is partly the imputation
constant.

As the Q1 consequence (unchanged transport) on the imputed
cohort, the
scorecard's high-risk tier (score $\ge +1$) missed 47 of 188 cancers

\subsection{Calibration and missing-data checks}
\label{sec:calibration}

For calibration on the imputed cohort, the recalibrated
scorecard had Brier 0.097, slope 1.00 (guaranteed by the refit---the
asymmetry flagged in Section~\ref{sec:results}), and ECE 0.042,
versus its frozen map (0.116, 2.02, 0.128), the frozen logistic
regression (0.111, 1.44, 0.135), ROMA (0.209, 1.53, 0.220), and
CPH-I (0.259, 1.11, 0.303), the formulas under-predicting risk
(CITL $-0.95$, $-1.42$).
On the 100 complete cases (all models frozen), Brier/slope/ECE were
0.077/1.77/0.084 (scorecard), 0.105/1.19/0.130 (logistic
regression), 0.154/1.77/0.212 (ROMA), and 0.211/0.99/0.272
(CPH-I): the frozen scorecard had the lowest Brier and ECE on
measured biology, though its slope was the farthest from 1.
Refitting without HE4 gave 0.925 (scorecard), 0.932 (logistic),
0.891 (XGBoost, defaults). The MICE-style imputation
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) degraded the
scorecard's tertile binning and reversed the ensemble ordering, the
logistic regression highest throughout; the full MICE AUROC set (Setting
B trees) was 0.926
(logistic regression), 0.865 (CatBoost), 0.856 (Random Forest), 0.853
(scorecard), 0.848 (CPH-I), 0.818 (XGBoost), 0.817 (ROMA), and 0.642
(CA125 rule).

\subsection{Robustness and simulation}

Under 30\% multiplicative noise (West China, $N=380$; ensembles at
package defaults), AUROC losses
were 0.040 (scorecard), 0.002 (logistic regression),
0.074 (ROMA), and 0.086 (XGBoost). Across the full robustness
grid of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} (10\%/30\%
multiplicative/additive/combined noise, 5\% gross-error
contamination), scorecard 0.869--0.921, logistic regression
0.932--0.936, ROMA 0.791--0.871, CPH-I 0.839--0.868, ensembles
0.775--0.882.
Shifting the frozen cutpoints by $\pm10\%$ moved the
scorecard's AUROC by at most 0.016 (0.913--0.929); retraining on
60--349 patients (ten draws) gave, at full size,
logistic regression 0.936, scorecard 0.929, ensembles 0.878--0.887.

minus scorecard test AUROC at 50\% noise (12 of 24 cells per
drift level); red favors the ensembles, blue the scorecard. Axes:
$n\in\{100,200,400,800\}$; shift $s\in\{0,0.5,1.0\}$.}
\label{fig:regime}

In the simulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:regime}), the ensemble-average
minus-scorecard gap averaged $+0.015$ at $d=0$ of \eqref{eq:eta} and
$-0.015$ at $d=1$ across all 24 cells ($+0.018$/$-0.018$ in the
50\%-noise slice shown, 12 of 24 cells per
drift level); at $d=1$ the scorecard had the highest AUROC in 22 of
24 cells. That regime favors the scorecard by construction---at
$d=1$, \eqref{eq:eta} is additive in the three features, removing
the interactions and nonlinearity that flexible models learn (the
scorecard's bins are additive too)---so the cells measure loss
of the ensembles' learned structure, not scorecard superiority.
The
XGBoost-minus-scorecard gap's spread (max minus min of factor-level means) was largest
across drift (0.033), then shift (0.012), training size (0.003),
and noise (0.002).

\subsection{Internal validity and sensitivity}
\label{sec:internal}

Across 20 repeated five-fold cross-validations, internal AUROCs were
0.906 (SD 0.003; scorecard), 0.894 (logistic regression), and
0.882--0.900 (default ensembles); nested-CV tuning gave 0.885,
0.903, 0.900.
The deployed three-bin configuration was selected in 18 of 20
repeats; ensembles fitted in-sample at 0.991--1.000, falling
0.091--0.118 under CV. There was no internal advantage for the
flexible models to lose: internal-minus-external deltas on the
imputed West China cohort were
$-0.023$ (scorecard), $-0.042$ (logistic
regression), $-0.005$ (XGBoost), $+0.008$ (Random Forest), and
$+0.023$ (CatBoost), and \cite{christodoulou2019} reports the same
null result across clinical prediction models generally.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

Three results follow. First, complexity did not consistently
improve external performance, but neither
did the scorecard beat the simpler linear models. Seven integers
matched the logistic regression and the formulas: the scorecard
was numerically best on the complete cases (0.971, a
63\%-prevalence estimate), not significantly differing
from the logistic regression, ROMA, or
XGBoost; the logistic regression led the imputed
cohort and CPH-I the Japan test.
The scorecard's honest comparator is the logistic
regression, which ties or beats it on the imputed cohort, under MICE,
under 30\% noise, and in minimum harm at every weight; the scorecard's gain is
hand-computability, at a small robustness cost.
Second, on decisions: the published cutpoints missed 82 (ROMA, an
imputation artifact) and
48 (CPH-I) of 188 imputed-cohort cancers versus 47
for the scorecard's tier; recalibrated, the
scorecard stayed lowest among the three rules to 10:1, the logistic
regression lowest overall to $w=10$. Third, the simulation is supporting evidence
only---one plausible mechanism (non-persisting learned interactions)
by which flexible models lose their advantage under drift; the
22-of-24 result at $d=1$ is close to built in (the drift-removed
logit is additive in the features).

Limitations: (i) all three cohorts are Asian hospital series; (ii)
HE4 was missing for 74\% of West China patients and age for 85\% of
Japan patients; with HE4 fixed at its training median, ROMA's
$N=380$ sensitivity (56.4\%) and AUROC (0.886) are imputation
artifacts; the strongest result rests on 100 measured-HE4 patients; the Japanese controls are healthy volunteers rather than
women with masses; (iii) West China's 49.5\% prevalence far exceeds referral
populations' 10--25\%~\cite{rolfsen2020}, tying
Table~\ref{tab:harm}/Fig.~\ref{fig:cons} to it; (iv) no assay platform
is named: the 13.1\%/27.7\% cutoffs are Fujirebio-specific
(ARCHITECT: 7.4\%/25.3\%), as are the tertiles;
(v) no ultrasound; (vi) the simulation is a simplification; here the
scorecard matched the logistic regression and the formulas, beat two
of the three tuned ensembles on the outcome-selected complete cases,
hand-computability its asset.
